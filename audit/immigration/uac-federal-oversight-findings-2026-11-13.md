# Federal Unaccompanied Children Oversight Audit — Initial Findings

**Audit date:** 2026-11-13  
**Scope:** Federal Unaccompanied Children (UC/UAC) program; ICE/HSI, HHS/ORR/ACF, DOJ/EOIR, DHS OIG and HHS OIG  
**Status:** Preliminary desk audit. No agency FOIA request has been submitted. No child-level records have been obtained or independently reconciled.

## Executive finding

The public record establishes serious federal weaknesses in tracking, interagency information exchange, sponsor safety checks, and post-release follow-up for unaccompanied children. It does **not** establish that 448,000–500,000 children were all missing, trafficked, or lost, nor does it establish where each child is today.

The most defensible starting cohort is the **448,000+ children ICE transferred to HHS/ORR during FY2019–FY2023**. That is a transfer population, not a missing-child count. The oft-repeated "half a million missing children" framing collapses multiple distinct statuses—no Notice to Appear, missed hearing, incomplete address, runaway from care, inability to contact, and unknown current location—into one unsupported category.

A separate claim requires direct substantiation: ICE leadership testified on February 10, 2026 that DHS/HSI and HHS had located **more than 145,000** children since February 2025, after **more than 71,000 field engagements**. The hearing record does not itself define the numerator, unique-child deduplication, source cohorts, successful-contact standard, safety verification, reunification, or other disposition. It does not show how many of the 145,000 came from the 448,000 FY2019–FY2023 audit cohort. That claim is now a primary records target.

## 1. What DHS OIG actually found

### OIG-24-46 management alert (August 19, 2024)

DHS OIG reported that ICE had transferred more than 448,000 unaccompanied children to HHS from FY2019 through FY2023. ICE reported more than 32,000 missed immigration-court appearances in that period, and OIG said ICE could not account for the location of all children released by HHS who did not appear. The alert recommended an automated system for court appearances/address information and a formal process for identifying missed appearances and sharing those cases with HHS.

Source: https://www.oversight.gov/reports/audit/management-alert-ice-cannot-monitor-all-unaccompanied-migrant-children-released-dhs

### OIG-25-21 final audit (March 25, 2025)

The final report updated key figures and found:

- **448,000+** transfers from ICE to HHS during FY2019–FY2023; most children were later released to sponsors.
- **31,000+ (about 7%)** release addresses were blank, undeliverable, or missing apartment numbers. OIG described two addresses that did not exist despite 34 children being associated with those sponsor addresses.
- ICE was not always aware of the location of children who fled HHS custody.
- As of **January 2025**, ICE had not served Notices to Appear (NTAs) on **233,000+** children.
- As of **October 2024**, ICE reported **43,000+** children who had been served NTAs failed to appear for scheduled court dates.
- ICE was not always notified of children’s status or safety unless an allegation or tip was reported.

These are different administrative categories. An unserved NTA is not a missed hearing. A missed hearing is not proof that the child is physically missing. An incomplete address is a serious tracking defect, but not proof that a child was harmed. A runaway from an ORR facility is distinct from a child released to a sponsor and later unreachable.

Sources:
- Full report: https://www.oig.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/assets/2025-03/OIG-25-21-Mar25.pdf
- Oversight.gov report record: https://www.oversight.gov/reports/audit/ice-cannot-effectively-monitor-location-and-status-all-unaccompanied-alien-children
- DHS OIG testimony, July 23, 2025: https://oversight.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Cuffari-Written-Testimony.pdf

### OIG-25-21 causes identified

DHS OIG identified incomplete and outdated interagency agreements; limited exchange of sponsor address changes and welfare information; no formal proactive monitoring procedures; insufficient dedicated staffing; and weak operational guidance. OIG also said ICE did not have an established relationship with the Department of Labor for labor-exploitation information.

The July 23, 2025 Inspector General testimony said ICE had implemented a process to share court-appearance information with HHS, closing that earlier management-alert recommendation, while the earlier recommendation for an automated court/address system remained open at that time. The testimony described the four final-audit recommendations as still in implementation, with full implementation expected during 2025.

## 2. What ICE later claimed it did

At the February 10, 2026 House Committee on Homeland Security hearing, ICE senior official Todd Lyons stated that since February 2025:

- HSI conducted **71,000+ field engagements** to locate children, verify sponsor legitimacy, and uncover criminal activity related to exploitation.
- ICE had arrested **500+ criminal sponsors** for offenses including child sexual exploitation, forced labor, identity fraud, immigration violations, and aggravated assault.
- HSI had dismantled **2,400+ human-smuggling operations**, including nearly 730 criminal prosecutions across listed offense categories.
- DHS law enforcement and HHS had **located 145,000+ children**.

Primary source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-119hhrg63777/html/CHRG-119hhrg63777.htm (prepared statement and testimony of Todd M. Lyons, especially the February 10, 2026 statement).

### What is not established by that testimony

The cited testimony does not provide:
1. Whether 145,000 means unique children, visits, successful door knocks, address verifications, or a mixture of outcomes.
2. The source cohort and exact date range for each child.
3. How many were from the 448,000 FY2019–FY2023 OIG cohort.
4. How many were found at the original sponsor address, at a changed address, in ORR care, in another government custody, or through a third party.
5. How many had a documented direct safety/well-being check with the child, rather than contact with a sponsor or household member.
6. How many were referred for trafficking, abuse, neglect, or labor investigations; how many allegations were substantiated; and what the case dispositions were.
7. Whether located children were reunified with family, remained with sponsors, entered another care arrangement, reached adulthood, left the country, were detained, or were removed.
8. Whether the 500+ sponsor arrests and 2,400+ operations overlap with the 145,000 cohort, and whether arrest means charge, indictment, conviction, or another arrest event.

**Audit conclusion:** the 145,000 claim is an official statement made to Congress, but its underlying counting method and outcome evidence must be obtained before it can be treated as a verified recovery count. Do not subtract 145,000 from 448,000 to estimate a remaining missing population; the denominators and categories have not been reconciled.

## 3. New HHS OIG evidence (September 2026)

HHS OIG report OEI-07-24-00370, *In 2024, ORR’s National Call Center Connected Children with Supports and Services*, is a current, important source. It states that approximately **213,000** unaccompanied children were released from ORR care to sponsors during FY2023 and FY2024.

For January 1–June 30, 2024, the National Call Center received **1,944 unique reports** of potential safety concerns, representing approximately **1,800 released children**. Categories included:
- Unable to contact: 735
- Runaway: 528
- Child abuse/neglect allegations: 214
- Trafficking concerns: 184
- Sponsor-child conflict: 47
- CPS intervention: 34
- Child sexual-abuse allegation: 27

In a random sample of 250 reports concerning runaway, abuse/neglect, or trafficking concerns:
- The call center attempted to contact children in 70% of applicable cases and reached children in 61% of those attempts.
- It referred 82% of cases to at least one other entity.
- It conducted follow-up calls in 42% of cases, although follow-up was not required during the review period.
- When it did not attempt contact, lack of child contact information was frequently cited.

The report does **not** count all children who were missing, nor does a potential concern equal confirmed victimization. It documents real follow-up and information gaps in a defined set of safety reports.

Primary source: https://oig.hhs.gov/documents/evaluation/11910/OEI-07-24-00370.pdf

The report also notes the National Call Center contractor changed on December 15, 2025. The current contractor's procedures, data continuity, quality controls, and performance should be requested.

## 4. HHS sponsor screening and post-release follow-up

HHS OIG's 2024 report reviewed ORR case files from the early-2021 surge period and found:
- One or more required sponsor safety checks lacked documentation in 16% of sampled children's files.
- For 19% of children released while FBI fingerprint or state child-abuse registry checks were pending, case files were never updated with results.
- Sponsor IDs had legibility concerns in 35% of files.
- Mandatory home studies were not conducted in two cases.
- Sponsor records were not updated with child-welfare outcomes or sponsorship history in 5% of cases.
- Timely Safety and Well-Being Follow-Up Calls were not conducted in 22% of cases; 18% had no documentation of follow-up calls.

These are sample case-file findings, not nationwide counts of children harmed. HHS OIG listed one sponsor-safety-check recommendation as still open/unimplemented with an update expected January 15, 2027; other listed recommendations had been closed as implemented.

Source: https://www.oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2024/gaps-in-sponsor-screening-and-followup-raise-safety-concerns-for-unaccompanied-children/

## 5. Recommendation-status reconciliation

There are two audit cycles and four final-audit recommendations; do not conflate them.

- The July 23, 2025 DHS OIG testimony says one OIG-24-46 recommendation on sharing court-appearance information with HHS had been closed after ICE implemented a process; the automated court/address system recommendation remained open at that date.
- Oversight.gov currently lists both OIG-24-46 recommendations as open. This conflicts with the Inspector General's July 2025 testimony and must be reconciled with DHS OIG's authoritative recommendation closeout record.
- Oversight.gov currently lists OIG-25-21 recommendations 2, 3, and 4 as closed. Recommendation 1 status was not confirmed in the public search conducted for this memo.
- Closure of a recommendation does not by itself prove the entire child population is now safely located or that the control works in practice. Obtain closure evidence and validation.

## 8. Safety and privacy controls

This project must not publish child names, A-numbers, dates of birth, sponsor names, sponsor addresses, phone numbers, shelter locations, or active investigative leads. Request aggregate tables, redacted records, data dictionaries, and methodology. If a record indicates a currently missing or endangered child, route it to the appropriate law-enforcement and child-protection authorities; do not post it publicly.

The audit is not an independent law-enforcement investigation and cannot determine a child's present location from public records. It can expose failures, test agency claims, identify unresolved cohorts, and demand accountable follow-through.

## 13. What the ICE Joint Initiative actually directed — operational reconstruction

The four-page ICE field-implementation document allows a more exact reconstruction of what the agency planned to do. It is not merely a general child-welfare tracing plan.

### Selection and prioritization

ICE said it assembled UAC information from multiple sources into a Power BI application and sorted the population into three priority groups:

- **"Flight Risk":** children with an in-absentia final removal order; children ORR could not contact after reunification/release; children who ran away from ORR facilities; and children released to sponsors not related by blood, including "super sponsors" (defined in the memo as an individual, address, or organization that sponsored more than three children).
- **"Public Safety":** categories including adults who fraudulently claimed to be UACs at entry, children ICE characterized as having gang/terrorist ties or activities, and children with arrests or serious criminal backgrounds.
- **"Border Security":** children with executable orders of removal, including voluntary departure, withdrawal, or removal orders.

These are the initiative's own administrative prioritization labels, not proof that a child in a category committed an offense, was trafficked, or was unsafe. Sponsor relationship alone is not evidence of abuse.

### Field execution and systems

The memo directed that:
- HSI support ERO; ERO officers experienced with UAC cases would lead locating and immigration-document actions.
- HSI and ERO field offices coordinate locally; HSI could prepare target packets and criminal checks, while ERO controlled immigration actions.
- HSI would verify whether a child was registered in school.
- HSI could coordinate with U.S. Attorney's Offices for warrants and with the U.S. Marshals Service on existing warrants.
- HSI/ERO should document any contact with a child and/or sponsor in a UAC Field Contact Documentation Form by 5 p.m. daily.
- HSI used its ICM system and a UAC Joint Initiative program code; ERO used EAGLE for enforcement actions and EARM for case-status updates.
- DHS's Center for Countering Human Trafficking would provide investigative and victim-protection expertise, process Continued Presence requests for identified trafficking victims, and maintain a leadership dashboard from daily updates.
- In exigent circumstances, that Center could assist with facial-recognition queries if the field office lacked access.

The memo expressly instructed field teams to pursue possible criminal charges and applicable immigration enforcement, including execution of final removal orders where possible. The document lists potential sponsor offenses for investigation, including false statements, smuggling/harboring, wire fraud, forced labor, sex trafficking, and conspiracy. Listing a possible charge is not evidence that a person was charged or convicted.

Primary document (public copy): https://wp-cpr.s3.amazonaws.com/uploads/2025/05/ICE-HSI-Cooperation-2025-Unaccompanied-Children.pdf

### What this proves and does not prove

**It proves the initiative's written design** included locating, school-registration checks, sponsor/household contact, criminal intelligence/investigations, immigration enforcement, field-contact documentation, and leadership reporting.

**It does not prove** how consistently field offices executed the plan, how many unique children were actually located, whether a child was directly contacted, whether a welfare assessment was completed, whether a trafficking concern was substantiated, or what happened after a contact. The public memo has no outcome table.

The National Immigration Project reports that it obtained this memo and a February 10, 2025 field-office coordination email through a FOIA request. It also reports practitioners' accounts of children experiencing ICE "wellness checks" as fear-inducing. That is an attributed advocacy/legal-practitioner account, not an independently established nationwide incidence estimate. The email reportedly required each field office to designate 1–2 officers. This prior production suggests the next request should seek later implementation versions and records created under the same program identifier.

Source: https://nipnlg.org/work/resources/end-sijs-backlog-coalition-foia-request-produces-ice-memos-unaccompanied-alien

## 14. Correct the "291,000 lost" claim — two different OIG snapshots

The August 19, 2024 DHS OIG management alert OIG-24-46 reported that as of May 2024 ICE had not served Notices to Appear on more than **291,000** UACs, meaning those children did not yet have an immigration court date. It separately reported more than **32,000** missed court appearances from FY2019–FY2023. It did not conclude that 291,000 children were physically missing.

The March 25, 2025 final audit OIG-25-21 used a later snapshot: more than **233,000** NTAs had not been served as of January 2025, and more than **43,000** children with served NTAs had failed to appear as of October 2024. The report still did not characterize all these children as physically missing.

Therefore:
- 291,000 = unserved NTA snapshot as of May 2024 (interim management alert).
- 233,000 = unserved NTA snapshot as of January 2025 (final audit).
- 32,000+ = missed hearings reported for FY2019–FY2023 (interim alert).
- 43,000+ = missed hearings reported as of October 2024 in the final audit.
- 448,000+ = transfers from ICE to HHS in FY2019–FY2023.

These are time-specific administrative counts, not additive missing-child totals. The 145,000 located claim must be crosswalked to a defined cohort and cannot be subtracted from any of these figures.

Sources:
- OIG-24-46: https://www.oig.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/assets/2024-08/OIG-24-46-Aug24.pdf
- OIG-25-21: https://www.oig.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/assets/2025-03/OIG-25-21-Mar25.pdf

## 17. September 2026 HHS OIG National Call Center evaluation — primary-source confirmation

On September 22, 2026, HHS OIG published evaluation **OEI-07-24-00370**, *In 2024, ORR's National Call Center Connected Children with Supports and Services*. This is a new, material source for the post-release safety side of the audit.

The official HHS OIG report page states that approximately **213,000 children were released from ORR care to sponsors during FY2023 and FY2024**. OIG reviewed all potential child-safety concerns reported to the National Call Center from **January 1 through June 30, 2024** and found nearly **2,000 reports representing approximately 1,800 released children**.

For its review of responses to reports concerning runaway, abuse/neglect, or trafficking concerns, OIG examined a **sample of cases** and reported:
- Contact attempts were made in 70% of cases; children were reached in 61% of those cases.
- Risk assessments were conducted in 80% of cases in which the call center spoke with children.
- Referrals for investigation or support were made in 82% of cases.
- Information about resources/community services was offered in 30% of cases.
- Follow-up calls occurred in 42% of cases. OIG notes follow-up was not required during the period reviewed.

OIG concluded that the call center took various actions to connect children with potential safety concerns to support, most commonly referrals to law enforcement and other entities. The report page does not state that these potential reports represent confirmed abuse, trafficking, or victimization. Nor does it establish the current whereabouts of children who could not be contacted. The report issued **no recommendations** according to HHS OIG's completed work-plan entry.

**Audit significance:** this is a defined and partially quantified view of reported post-release safety concerns. It does not provide a denominator for all released children who may have experienced harm, because the review begins with concerns reported to the call center. It also does not establish the ultimate disposition or resolution of every referral. The 1,800 children in this report must not be added to, or treated as a subset of, the 448,000+ ICE-to-HHS transfer cohort without a validated cohort crosswalk.

Primary sources:
- HHS OIG report page and report materials: https://www.oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2026/in-2024-orrs-national-call-center-connected-children-with-supports-and-services/
- HHS OIG completed work-plan entry: https://www.oig.hhs.gov/reports/work-plan/browse-work-plan-projects/review-of-the-office-of-refugee-resettlements-national-call-center-response-to-incoming-calls/
- Report highlights PDF: https://oig.hhs.gov/documents/evaluation/11911/OEI-07-24-00370-highlights.pdf

A June 12, 2025 DHS OIG management-response attachment records ICE's planned completion date of September 30, 2025 for recommendations 1–3. A planned completion date is not proof of implementation.

## 19. Revised investigative questions prompted by the HHS OIG findings

The HHS OIG evaluation narrows the next records requests. In addition to raw aggregate report counts, request:
1. The complete report-category definitions and data dictionary, including whether multiple reports can relate to one child and how duplicate reports are handled.
2. For each report category, counts of attempted child contact, successful direct child contact, sponsor-only contact, no contact information, risk assessment, referral, follow-up, and documented closure.
3. Referral destination and disposition categories, with personally identifying information redacted and child-level data withheld from public release.
4. The policy in force during January–June 2024 explaining when a call-center report was considered resolved, escalated, or referred, and whether the call center verified that a receiving agency accepted the referral.
5. The December 15, 2025 contractor transition records: continuity of case data, open-case handoff, quality assurance, call scripts, performance standards, and current follow-up requirements.
6. Whether the 2024 call-center cohort or any subsequent cohort is linked, through an authorized protected crosswalk, to ICE's 2025 Joint Initiative and the 145,000 located claim. Publish only aggregate reconciliations.

This creates a testable distinction between **report received**, **child reached**, **risk assessed**, **referral made**, **referral accepted**, and **safety outcome verified**. Those are separate events and must not be collapsed into a single "located" or "resolved" metric.


### 17A. Full-report denominator and referral details (verified against the HHS OIG PDF)

The full report clarifies several denominators that should accompany any use of the rounded highlights:

- The 1,944 figure is **unique reports**, not 1,944 phone calls and not 1,944 unique children. Those reports represented approximately 1,800 children. The call center handled more than **64,000 total contacts** (inbound/outbound calls, emails, and texts) during the same six-month period; one inquiry/report could involve multiple contacts.
- The 250-case random sample covered reports involving runaway, abuse/neglect, or trafficking concerns. These three categories represented 48% of all potential safety concerns in the six-month review.
- The call center spoke directly with children in **125 of 250 sampled cases (50%)**, including 33 cases in which the child initiated the call.
- For the other 217 cases where the initial caller was not the child, staff attempted to contact the child in **152 (70%)** and reached the child in **92 of those 152 attempts (60.5%)**.
- In the 65 cases without an attempted outgoing child contact, **40** records cited lack of contact information and **25** gave no reason. Staff were not required to document the reason for not contacting the child; therefore the 25 are a documentation gap, not proof that staff violated a contact requirement.
- Among the 125 cases in which staff spoke with a child, they conducted risk assessments in **100 (80%)**, trafficking screenings in **62 (49.6%)**, and provided personal safety plans in **38 (30.4%)**.
- Referrals were made in **205 of 250 cases (82%)**: 136 to local law enforcement, 111 to child protective services, 74 to NCMEC, and 61 to the Office on Trafficking in Persons. These referral categories overlap; they must not be summed as unique children or cases.
- Some receiving entities declined referrals because of jurisdiction, eligibility, or a requirement that referrals be made in person. An outbound referral is not proof that an investigation was opened or that the concern was resolved.
- Follow-up calls were made in **104 of 250 cases (41.6%)**. During the period reviewed, follow-up calls were not required by ORR policy. OIG did not assess whether the call center's individual response actions were appropriate and warned that actions not documented in the case-management system could cause undercounting.

**Current-policy development:** An endnote in the September 2026 report states that, as of August 2026, the then-current National Call Center contractor (Hendall) was required to conduct follow-up calls with a sponsor and/or child when notified that a child missed immigration court, and to create and execute follow-up procedures for any caller for whom a safety plan had been created. This is a material policy change from the 2024 review period. Request the current written policy, effective date, implementation evidence, and compliance data. Do not describe the 2024 41.6% follow-up rate as the current rate.

Primary source: full HHS OIG report, OEI-07-24-00370, methodology and Appendix A, especially PDF pages 4–14: https://oig.hhs.gov/documents/evaluation/11910/OEI-07-24-00370.pdf

**Interpretation boundary:** The 1,944 reports are an administrative signal of potential safety concerns, including inability to contact and runaway concerns. OIG did not verify the underlying safety concerns, did not evaluate whether each response was appropriate, and did not establish a current nationwide prevalence rate for abuse or trafficking. The report does establish that in the selected 250-case sample, direct contact, screenings, referrals, and follow-up were separate and variably documented actions; it does not establish ultimate safety outcomes for all children.


## 20. June 2026 HHS OIG audit — unlicensed care-provider background checks in Texas and Florida

A second 2026 HHS OIG report materially expands the safety audit beyond post-release tracing: **A-06-24-07001**, *The Office of Refugee Resettlement Needs To Improve Its Monitoring of Unlicensed Unaccompanied Alien Children Program Care Providers' Compliance With Background Check Requirements*, issued June 5, 2026.

### Verified findings

For the period September 2021 through August 2024, OIG reviewed 58 unlicensed ORR care-provider facilities in Texas and Florida. It found ORR conducted only **176 of 256 required monitoring visits**:
- **80 of 198** required quarterly abbreviated visits were not conducted.
- **43 of 58** required comprehensive visits were not conducted on time (required every two years).

OIG said missed or late monitoring visits meant missed opportunities to identify background-check noncompliance. New staff may have had access to children before providers verified that the staff member did not have a criminal history or other history indicating risk to children. OIG also noted a Florida obstacle to required background checks arising after the audit period.

The report explains that in January 2024 ORR officials disclosed that an unlicensed provider had not conducted required national FBI fingerprint-based background checks and had conducted only state-level checks. This is a documented control failure disclosure; it is not a finding that every facility or employee lacked checks, nor proof that a particular child was harmed.

### Corrective action status

HHS OIG lists both recommendations as **Open Unimplemented** in its tracker as of the current report page:
1. ORR should conduct required abbreviated monitoring visits approximately every 90 days.
2. ORR should put mechanisms in place to verify that all required background checks at unlicensed care providers are conducted.

The tracker lists the next update expected **December 4, 2026**. ACF partially concurred with recommendation 1 and concurred with recommendation 2.

### Audit significance and limitations

This report concerns children while in ORR-funded care, not the later ICE post-release field initiative. It is a separate stage of the custody chain and must not be combined with the 448,000 transfer cohort or the 1,800 children represented in National Call Center safety reports. It does establish that federal oversight of staff screening and provider monitoring had measurable gaps at 58 unlicensed facilities in two states during the stated period. The next audit question is whether corrective actions have been implemented and verified since August 2024, and whether the post-audit Florida background-check obstacle was resolved.

## 21. Contracting and provider oversight — 2021 emergency intake contracts

Two additional HHS OIG audits show that oversight gaps extended to the procurement and monitoring of emergency shelter capacity. These are relevant to the care system but are distinct from post-release child tracing.

### A-06-22-07000 — five emergency intake contracts (September 3, 2025)

In FY2021, ACF awarded **62 contracts totaling $4.8 billion** without competitive bidding for emergency intake sites. OIG selected five contracts totaling more than **$2.8 billion** for review and found noncompliance across contract award, monitoring, and closeout requirements:
- All five reviewed contracts lacked compliant planning for full and open competition.
- All five lacked a compliant pre-award determination that prices were fair and reasonable.
- Four of five had statements of work that did not align with performance outcomes.
- All five lacked required contract clauses.
- Four of five did not appropriately rely on quality-assurance surveillance plans.
- All five failed to comply with requirements to accept or reject contract deliverables.
- Three of five had applicable excess funds not properly deobligated; all five failed to obtain required releases of liabilities/obligations/claims before final payment.

OIG made four recommendations. HHS OIG's tracker lists all four as **Closed Unimplemented** (closed February 25, 2026). That label is not the same as "Implemented and Closed." It means the recommendations were administratively closed without being recorded as implemented in the tracker; do not describe these corrective actions as successfully implemented based on the closure date alone.

### A-03-22-00353 — $529 million Family Endeavors emergency intake site contract (February 10, 2026)

OIG found that ACF awarded a **$529 million sole-source contract** to Family Endeavors, Inc. for an emergency intake site in Pecos, Texas, three days after receiving an unsolicited proposal. ACF had anticipated the need for additional beds for months but did not adequately plan for competition. It could not document a pre-award price analysis; an analysis completed three months after award put ACF's total estimated cost at **$245 million**, less than half the awarded amount. ACF also did not evaluate whether the contractor was responsible before award. OIG concluded other, more qualified contractors may have been available at lower cost.

The report's single recommendation to use the findings to improve future contract planning is listed **Closed Implemented** on August 4, 2026. This closure concerns the forward-looking planning recommendation; it does not retroactively cure the original award deficiencies or establish that the $529 million contract was reasonable.
