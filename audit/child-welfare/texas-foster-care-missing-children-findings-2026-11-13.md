# CPS and Foster Care Missing-Children Oversight Audit — Texas and Federal Baseline

**Prepared:** 2026-11-13  
**Status:** Initial evidence baseline; not a completed case-file audit  
**Audit focus:** Children missing from foster care / state conservatorship, agency efforts to locate and protect them, required notifications, case closure while missing, and accuracy of missing-episode records.

## Executive boundary

This project audits agency systems and safeguarding performance. It does not publish, compile, infer, or attempt to identify individual children who may currently be missing. Identifiable case records belong with authorized child-protection and law-enforcement personnel. Public work will use aggregate/de-identified records, official audit findings, and process evidence. No child names, addresses, schools, placement locations, case numbers, images, or case-level identifying details are to be committed to this repository.

A child described as “recovered” in a fiscal-year total is not necessarily a child from the same fiscal-year missing cohort. Do not subtract recovered counts from missing counts unless DFPS confirms the cohort definitions and episode/unique-child rules. “Missing episodes” are not equivalent to unique children.

## Initial findings

### 1. Federal reporting failures — HHS OIG A-07-21-06102

HHS OIG's March 2, 2023 audit examined 74,353 foster-care missing-child episodes lasting at least two calendar days during July 1, 2018–December 31, 2020. It reviewed a stratified sample of 100 episodes:

- 33 episodes were reported to the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC) within 24 hours.
- 45 were never reported to NCMEC.
- 22 were reported at least two calendar days after the agency received notice.
- OIG estimated 51,115 of the 74,353 episodes were not reported in accordance with federal requirements: 34,869 never reported and 16,246 not reported within 24 hours.

These are OIG's sample-based national estimates, not a count of 51,115 distinct children and not a Texas-only estimate. OIG found state agencies generally lacked adequate systems to identify whether missing episodes were reported accurately and timely. ACF's recommendation to work with states was closed as implemented on March 20, 2024; closure of that recommendation does not itself prove state-level compliance in subsequent years.

Primary source: https://www.oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2023/state-agencies-did-not-always-ensure-that-children-missing-from-foster-care-were-reported-to-the-national-center-for-missing-and-exploited-children-in-accordance-with-federal-requirements/

### 2. Texas FY2023 missing-child report

Texas DFPS reported:

- 31,475 children/youth were in DFPS conservatorship at some point during FY2023.
- 1,164 unique children/youth were missing at some point during FY2023, including 83 already missing at the start of the fiscal year.
- 1,016 (87%) were located as of August 31, 2023.
- 109 (9%) remained missing and in DFPS conservatorship on August 31, 2023.
- 39 (3%) exited conservatorship while missing: 15 turned 18 while missing, 23 had legal responsibility terminated by a court before age 18, and one died while missing.
- Separately, DFPS reported 1,095 unique children recovered from missing status during FY2023. This is a fiscal-year recovery count and must not be treated as the exact recovery disposition of the 1,164-person cohort without reconciliation.

DFPS also reported that among the 1,095 unique children recovered during FY2023, 106 were identified as experiencing some form of victimization while missing; 53 were identified as sex-trafficking victims and one as a labor-trafficking victim. These are identified victims in DFPS's reported recovered population, not a complete measure of all victimization.

Primary sources:
- https://www.dfps.texas.gov/About_DFPS/Reports_and_Presentations/Agencywide/documents/2024/2024-05-31_Children_Youth_Missing_DFPS_Conservatorship_Human_Trafficking_Data_FY2023_Report.pdf
- https://www.dfps.texas.gov/About_DFPS/Reports_and_Presentations/Agencywide/documents/2024/2024-05-31_Children_Youth_Missing_DFPS_Conservatorship_Human_Trafficking_Data_FY2023_Report.pdf

### 3. Texas FY2024 agency-reported snapshot

DFPS's August 2025 Sunset self-evaluation reports:
- 935 children who went missing from DFPS conservatorship during FY2024.
- 919 children missing from DFPS conservatorship “recovered.”
- 192 cases categorized as suspected/unconfirmed and/or confirmed trafficking.

The report excerpt does not establish whether the 919 recovered are the same FY2024 cohort, whether the counts are unique children or episodes in every field, how many remained missing at year-end, or the disposition of children who exited conservatorship while missing. The 192 trafficking field explicitly combines suspected/unconfirmed and confirmed cases. Do not label all 192 as confirmed trafficking victims. These limitations are central audit questions.

Primary source: https://www.dfps.texas.gov/About_DFPS/Reports_and_Presentations/Agencywide/documents/2025/DFPS_SunsetReview_2025.pdf (August 2025, p.119)

### 4. Texas historical concern: ending conservatorship while a child remains missing

DFPS's FY2023 report documents 23 children whose legal responsibility was terminated by a court before age 18 while they were still missing. Texas Public Radio's 2022 reporting described 42 minors in one year for whom the state sought termination while missing, and 40 missing-minor cases reportedly closed in 2021. The reporting is a lead for records review, not proof of improper motive or unlawful action. The agency and court records must be reviewed case by case by authorized auditors, using de-identified identifiers in public outputs.

Sources:
- DFPS FY2023 report above.
- https://www.tpr.org/government-politics/2022-07-14/texas-foster-care-ends-its-stewardship-when-some-youth-run-away
- https://www.tpr.org/government-politics/2023-03-20/texas-washing-hands-of-runaway-and-missing-foster-kids

### 5. Comparator: Alaska HHS OIG 2026 review

HHS OIG's 2026 review of American Indian and Alaska Native children missing from Alaska foster care found:
- In 25% of incidents, Alaska did not take additional steps beyond notifications to locate and return children as required.
- In nearly 60% of incidents, required caseworker visits had not all occurred before the child went missing.
- In 38% of children reviewed, additional missing incidents were not documented in the state's case-management system.
- OIG found gaps in notifications, trafficking assessments, post-return inquiries, and communication with Tribes.
- All three OIG recommendations to ACF were open/unimplemented on the report page at the time reviewed.

This is an Alaska finding, not evidence that Texas has the same failure rate. It supplies specific control tests to apply in Texas.

Primary source: https://www.oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2026/alaska-missed-opportunities-to-protect-american-indian-and-alaska-native-children-missing-from-foster-care/

### 6. Current federal work plan

HHS OIG announced an active May 15, 2026 review of Mississippi's efforts to protect children missing from foster care. It plans to review case files, prevention efforts, locating activity, and services after return. This offers a model for federal follow-up and potential cross-state comparison.

Primary source: https://www.oig.hhs.gov/reports/work-plan/browse-work-plan-projects/mississippis-efforts-to-protect-children-missing-from-foster-care/

## Audit questions to answer

1. For each missing episode, what is the exact date/time the child was last seen, the date/time DFPS learned the child was missing, and the date/time each required report was made?
2. Was NCMEC notified within the applicable 24-hour requirement? Was law enforcement notified? Were parents/guardians, attorneys ad litem, courts, placement providers, tribes (where applicable), and other required parties notified?
3. What documented search and recovery actions occurred, by whom, and when? Were additional actions taken for high-risk or repeat-missing children?
4. Were required caseworker visits completed before the missing event? Were safety plans and placement risk assessments current?
5. Was a trafficking-risk assessment completed when required? After return, was the child interviewed privately and offered medical, behavioral-health, advocacy, and other needed services?
6. Were all episodes recorded in the state system and reconciled to NCMEC and law-enforcement records? Are repeated missing episodes collapsed or omitted?
7. Did a child remain missing at fiscal-year end? Did the child turn 18, die, or have conservatorship terminated while still missing? Was a court order obtained, and what contemporaneous evidence supported the state's action?
8. Are the agency's public totals internally consistent across annual reports, Sunset submissions, federal AFCARS/NCANDS-related reporting, NCMEC, and court data?
9. What placement conditions, instability, placement disruptions, staffing/caseload issues, or service gaps preceded repeat missing episodes? Do not infer causation without case-level evidence.
10. Were agency recommendations closed based on training/policy issuance alone, or on tested operational evidence showing timely notifications and documented locating activity?

## Proposed audit method

- Build a source register and year-by-year aggregate dataset before drawing conclusions.
- Obtain de-identified episode-level extracts from DFPS for FY2019–FY2025, with stable random audit IDs (not case numbers or hashes of identifying data).
- Reconcile DFPS episode records against NCMEC notification timestamps, law-enforcement notifications, placement records, caseworker contacts, court actions, and post-return services.
- Use a stratified sample: still missing at year-end; exited conservatorship while missing; repeat missing; trafficking-risk flag; high-risk classification; and apparently routine recovered episodes.
- Validate the sampling frame and all denominator definitions. Distinguish child counts, episode counts, recoveries during period, cohort outcomes, and end-of-period status.
- Conduct independent re-performance of notification timeliness and search-step compliance.
- Preserve adverse, exculpatory, and ambiguous evidence; request agency response before publishing findings.
- Public dashboard: aggregate counts and control performance only. No individual child-level rows or identifying attributes.

## Initial priority

The highest-value Texas records are: the complete FY2024 and FY2025 missing-youth episode extracts with definitions; the FY2023 109 still-missing and 23 court-terminated-under-18 dispositions; all de-identified records of conservatorship termination while missing; and timestamped proof of NCMEC/law-enforcement notifications and search actions.

**No current missing-child roster was accessed. No individual child has been identified or located through this audit.** This repository work is a public-accountability audit, not a substitute for law enforcement or NCMEC recovery operations.


## 7. Updated 2025–2026 evidence and Texas legal control tests

### NCMEC 2025 Impact Report — national missing-from-care reports
NCMEC reports **23,348 reports of children missing from foster care in 2025**. Of the children missing from care who were reported to NCMEC in 2025, **17% were identified as likely victims of child sex trafficking**. These are NCMEC report counts, not necessarily unique children, not a census of every child missing from care nationwide, and not confirmed trafficking victim counts. NCMEC separately reports 4,215 instances of support for children missing from child welfare who were likely victims of sex trafficking.

NCMEC says Texas saw the largest single-year volume increase in missing-child reports in 2025, driven by new reporting legislation; this should not be interpreted as proof that more children went missing. It is at least partly a reporting-volume effect.

Source: https://www.missingkids.org/ourwork/impact (2025 Impact Report, sections “Children Missing from Foster Care” and “Emerging Trend | Texas Reporting Legislation Increases Reporting of Missing Children”).

### Texas law effective September 1, 2025
Texas Code of Criminal Procedure Article 63.00905, as amended by HB 908, requires a law-enforcement agency receiving a missing-child report to immediately investigate and, within two hours, enter applicable information into the Texas clearinghouse/TLETS, NCIC (if criteria are met), and NCMEC. Article 63.0091 establishes enhanced response procedures for certain repeat-missing children, including a child in foster care or DFPS conservatorship reported missing at least twice in the preceding 24 months. The audit must test actual operational compliance with these provisions, not infer compliance from the existence of the statute.

Official statute: https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CR/htm/CR.63.htm

### DFPS Missing Children Protocol — November 2025
DFPS's November 2025 Missing Children Protocol provides the agency's current process rules, including escalation when law enforcement declines to file a missing-child report. The protocol directs the caseworker to immediately notify the chain of command and include the Special Investigations program director; the director must request that Texas DPS place the child on the Child Safety Check Alert List. The protocol also includes special handling for youth parents and their children.

Source: https://www.dfps.texas.gov/handbooks/CPS/Resource_Guides/Missing_Children_Protocol.pdf

### Texas Child Welfare Bench Book — September 2025
The Texas Children's Commission's 2025 Bench Book summarizes DFPS duties to report missing foster youth to law enforcement and NCMEC within 24 hours, notify specified parties, continue locating efforts (including monthly law-enforcement and former-caregiver contacts), conduct quarterly supervisory reviews for children age 15 or younger, and complete a recovery interview after return. The quarterly-review age threshold is explicit in Texas Family Code §264.123(d)(2): 15 years of age or younger. The earlier draft's “15 and older” wording was incorrect and is superseded here. The audit should compare these controls with actual timestamped case records and applicable statutory/policy versions.

Source: https://www.texaschildrenscommission.gov/media/4sahodqj/2025-texas-child-welfare-bench-book-online.pdf

## 8. New highest-priority tests
1. Determine whether Texas's 2025 two-hour law-enforcement-to-NCMEC requirement is being met for foster-care missing-child reports, and reconcile timestamps among local law enforcement, TLETS, NCIC, NCMEC, and DFPS.
2. Test Article 63.0091 enhanced response for foster-care children with at least two missing reports in 24 months.
3. Verify the November 2025 DFPS Missing Children Protocol was distributed, trained, version-controlled, and operationalized statewide.
4. Test whether every missing episode has required party notifications, monthly law-enforcement/relative/former-caregiver/social-service contacts, quarterly supervisory reviews for children age 15 or younger, and a recovery interview (subject to the statutory abduction-investigation exception).
5. Reconcile NCMEC's 23,348 missing-from-care reports and 17% likely trafficking indicator with state data without treating reports as unique children or likely trafficking as confirmed victimization.
